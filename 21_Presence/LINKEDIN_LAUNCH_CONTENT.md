### Post 10 — Thu, week 3
**Pillar:** Revenue Beyond Money · **House:** Frameworks · **Format:** Framework Drop

> If you only measure money, you'll miss the revenue keeping you alive.
>
> Five forms, and most organisations track exactly one:
>
> **1. Monetary** — cash in. The most visible and the most fragile, because it's an *output* of the other four.
>
> **2. Relational** — trust, referral networks, strategic relationships. Doesn't appear on the P&L until suddenly it does.
>
> **3. Reputational** — authority and credibility. Either earning you opportunities or costing you them. There's no neutral setting.
>
> **4. Operational** — efficiency and waste removed. Money not spent is revenue earned, and it's the form most often filed under "cost of doing business."
>
> **5. Strategic** — a partnership, a pivot, a positioning change. Rare, slow, and capable of outproducing a year of sales activity.
>
> This applies well beyond companies chasing profit. A non-profit's revenue is mission sustainability. A partnership's revenue is mutual benefit. The form changes; the principle doesn't.
>
> Score yourself 1–5 on each. The gap between your highest and your lowest is usually exactly where the leak is.
>
> Which one is your lowest, and how long has it been that way?

> Revenue growth is not the goal.
>
> I know how that sounds coming from someone whose entire business is revenue. Stay with me.
>
> Growth is an amplifier. It takes whatever your system currently does and does more of it. If the system is sound, growth compounds. If the system is broken, growth just gets you to the failure faster and with more people watching.

> I've watched companies hit a number they'd chased for two years and discover it made everything worse: delivery couldn't hold, the best people left, margin inverted, and the churn they'd been outrunning finally caught them.

> The goal is a system that produces revenue *repeatably*. Growth is what that system does on its own once it works.
>
> Which means the honest question before any growth push isn't "how do we get more?"
>
> It's: **if this doubled next quarter, what breaks first?**
>
> You almost certainly already know the answer. Most people do.
>
> So — what breaks first?

### Post 14 — Thu, week 4
**Pillar:** Revenue Intelligence · **House:** Frameworks · **Format:** Framework Drop
**Economic job:** Diagnostic framing → the audit · **Source:** Draft 13 revenue-anatomy structure

> You have one revenue number. You need four.
>
> "Revenue was up 12%" tells you almost nothing about the health of the business underneath it. Split it:
>
> **New** — first-time customers. The most expensive and the most celebrated. Also the most volatile.
>
> **Expansion** — existing customers buying more. The cheapest revenue you will ever earn, and usually the least deliberately pursued.
>
> **Retention** — revenue that simply didn't leave. Invisible when it's working. Catastrophic when it stops.
>
> **Reactivation** — customers who left and came back. Almost nobody has a system for this, and almost everybody has the list.
>
> Now look at your 12% again. If all of it is New while Retention quietly slipped, you're not growing — you're refilling a bucket faster than it drains, and paying full acquisition price to do it.
>
> Two companies with identical revenue and identical growth rates can have completely different futures, and the single number hides which one you are.
>
> Pull the four for your last twelve months. It takes an afternoon.
>
> Which of the four is doing the most work in your business — and which one have you never deliberately worked at all?

## 7. Revenue Architecture Brief #001 — full draft

**Title:** Tool Adoption Without Process Design
**Format:** framework → evidence → model → methodology → perspective → CTA
**Withheld deliberately:** the 90-point scorecard's category weights. Publish the model's *shape*, never its calibration — the calibration is the Gateway Offer.

> **Revenue Architecture Brief #001**
> **Tool Adoption Without Process Design**
>
> **The pattern**
>
> Across sectors, one failure repeats more than any other in revenue tooling. It is not underinvestment, and it is not choosing the wrong vendor. It is buying software to resolve a process decision that has not been made.
>
> The sequence is consistent: a problem becomes visible; software is purchased against it; the software arrives with a process model already embedded, designed for a company that is not yours; the existing workflow is never redesigned to meet it; adoption stalls somewhere below a third of capability; the tool is blamed and eventually replaced.
>
> **What it costs**
>
> The licence is the smallest cost. The real costs are the migration effort spent twice, the reporting built on a system nobody fully uses, the credibility spent internally on a change that visibly didn't land, and the organisational conclusion — usually wrong — that the category doesn't work here.
>
> The most expensive outcome is the confidence lost. After two failed rollouts, the next genuinely correct tool is much harder to fund.
>
> **The model**
>
> Every tool encodes three things: a data model, a workflow, and an assumption about who does what. A purchase succeeds when all three match a process you have already defined. It fails when the tool is expected to supply the definition.
>
> Software cannot decide who owns a stage, what "qualified" means in your business, or which handoff is allowed to fail. Those are management decisions. A tool that arrives before they're made simply makes the ambiguity more expensive and harder to see.
>
> **The diagnostic**
>
> One question, applied before any purchase:
>
> *Can you describe the process this tool will run — on paper, in steps, with named owners and exit criteria — without naming the tool?*
>
> If the description requires the tool's vocabulary, the process doesn't exist independently of the tool, and you are buying a decision rather than an instrument.
>
> Three supporting checks:
>
> 1. **Ownership** — does every step have exactly one accountable name?
> 2. **Exit criteria** — could a stranger verify that a step is complete?
> 3. **Failure path** — what is supposed to happen when a step doesn't happen?
>
> A process that passes all three can be implemented in almost any competent tool. A process that fails them will not be rescued by any of them.
>
> **Perspective**
>
> This is why "which tool should we use?" is nearly always the wrong opening question, and why the answer so often disappoints. The tool is the last decision in the sequence, not the first. Most stacks are not overbuilt — they are under-decided.
>
> **What to do next**
>
> Take the last three tools your organisation bought. For each, write the process on paper without naming the tool. The ones you cannot write are the ones to address before the next purchase.
>
> — Arika Agency
