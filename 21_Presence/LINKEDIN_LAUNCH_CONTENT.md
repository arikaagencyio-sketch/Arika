**Pillar:** Revenue Reality · **House:** Founder Thinking · **Format:** Opinion Stand

**Funnel stage:** Problem awareness · **Source:** the repo's own state

> I built 106 AI agents for my agency.
>
> Not one of them can run.
>
> The API key isn't set. The runtime was never booted. Every one of them is a specification sitting in a folder, waiting for infrastructure that doesn't exist yet.
>
> I did the fun part first.
>
> I want to be precise about why that matters, because it isn't a story about AI.
>
> It's the same mistake I watch companies make with revenue every week:
>
> — The CRM before the process it's supposed to hold
> — The sales hire before the offer they're supposed to sell
> — The campaign before the thing it's supposed to point at
> — The automation before the workflow it's supposed to remove
>
> Architecture is satisfying. It feels like progress because it looks like progress.
>
> Execution isn't satisfying. It's small, repetitive and unglamorous, and it's the only thing that produces revenue.
>
> I'm not going to pretend I've solved this. I'm going to fix it in public, starting this week.
>
> What's the thing you've architected beautifully and never turned on?

### Post 2 — Wed, week 1
**Pillar:** Revenue Intelligence · **House:** Frameworks · **Format:** Framework Drop
**Economic job:** Demonstrate diagnostic capability → diagnostic conversation
**Funnel stage:** Problem awareness · **Source:** Sector strategic intelligence node — *"CRM data quality is a mirror of sales culture, not a tech failure"*

> Your CRM isn't dirty because your team is lazy.
>
> It's dirty because nobody ever told them what it's for.
>
> CRM data quality isn't a tech problem. It's a mirror of sales culture.
>
> Look at what a messy CRM is actually telling you:
>
> — Fields nobody fills in are fields nobody is measured on
> — Deals stuck in one stage mean the stage has no exit criteria
> — Duplicate records mean two people don't know they're working the same account
> — Notes written after the fact mean the rep is reporting, not thinking
>
> None of that is fixed by better software. You can migrate a bad culture into a beautiful new system over a weekend, and by Friday it looks exactly the same.
>
> The fix is upstream:
>
> 1. Decide what the CRM is *for* — forecasting, coaching, or handoffs. Pick one primary.
> 2. Give every stage an exit criterion a stranger could verify.
> 3. Measure the behaviour, not the field.
> 4. Delete every field that doesn't feed a decision.
>
> Then the data gets clean, because it finally has a job.
>
> Which of those four is missing in yours right now?

### Post 3 — Fri, week 1
**Pillar:** Revenue Architecture · **House:** Insights · **Format:** Hook-and-Pivot
**Economic job:** Position the agency as the filter between problem and purchase
**Funnel stage:** Problem awareness · **Source:** Sector cross-sector pattern — *"tool adoption without process design"*

> You have 14 tools. You use 3.
>
> That's not experimentation. That's eleven unfinished decisions you're paying rent on.
>
> The most common pattern I see across sectors isn't underinvestment in tooling. It's tool adoption without process design.
>
> It goes like this:
>
> A problem shows up. Someone buys software at it. The software arrives with someone else's process baked in. Nobody redesigns the actual workflow. The tool gets used at about 20% and blamed at 100%.
>
> Six months later the conclusion is "that tool didn't work for us."
>
> The tool was never the variable.
>
> Here's the test before your next purchase:
>
> Can you describe the process the tool will run — on paper, in steps, with owners — without naming the tool?
>
> If you can't, you're not buying a tool. You're buying a decision you haven't made yet, and hoping it arrives with the license.
>
> What's sitting in your stack right now that you'd struggle to defend?

### Post 4 — Mon, week 2
**Pillar:** Revenue Operations · **House:** Founder Thinking · **Format:** Narrative Lesson
**Economic job:** Open the reliability/monitoring surface (Automation 16)
**Funnel stage:** Problem awareness → solution awareness · **Source:** the real 11-day outage

> My agency's only live automation died.
>
> I found out 11 days later.
>
> Not because anyone complained. Not because anything crashed loudly. It just stopped — and everything downstream of it quietly stopped too, and every dashboard I had said nothing at all.
>
> Eleven days.
>
> What bothered me wasn't the failure. Things fail. What bothered me was that nothing was watching.
>
> I'd built the pipeline. I had not built the thing that tells you the pipeline is dead.
>
> This is the most common gap I find in revenue systems, and it's almost never the one people ask about:
>
> — The lead form that silently stopped delivering
> — The sequence that's been sending to a dead list since March
> — The integration that failed auth and never told anyone
> — The report that's been pulling from a broken join for a quarter
>
> None of those announce themselves. You find out from a customer, or from a month-end number that makes no sense.
>
> Every automation you own needs three things: a heartbeat, an owner, and an alert when the heartbeat stops.
>
> Mine now has all three. It cost me 11 days to learn that.
>
> What's running in your business right now that nobody would notice had stopped?

### Post 5 — Wed, week 2
**Pillar:** Revenue Reality · **House:** Insights · **Format:** Opinion Stand
**Economic job:** Pre-hire audit → the Gateway diagnostic
**Funnel stage:** Solution awareness · **Source:** Draft 13 "The Salesperson Trap" (argument retained, autobiography removed)

> Hiring a salesperson doesn't fix your revenue.
>
> It exposes every gap you refused to fix — faster, and with someone else's career attached to it.
>
> A salesperson is an accelerant. That's the whole job. They take whatever exists and make more of it happen.
>
> So if what exists is:
>
> — an offer people can't immediately price against the outcome
> — a market you can only describe as "everyone"
> — no repeatable way to get in front of that market
> — no idea what a buyer must believe before they say yes
>
> …then you didn't hire a solution. You hired a magnifier, pointed it at the problem, and attached a quota to it.
>
> Six months later the conclusion is "they weren't the right fit." Sometimes true. Usually not.
>
> Before the next hire, answer four questions in one sentence each:
>
> Who do we serve? What exactly do they get? How do they find us? What must they believe first?
>
> If any of those takes a paragraph, fix that before you post the role.
>
> Which one takes you a paragraph?

### Post 6 — Fri, week 2
**Pillar:** Revenue Signals · **House:** Frameworks · **Format:** Framework Drop
**Economic job:** Route to the Revenue Infrastructure Audit (Gateway Offer, 14)
**Funnel stage:** Solution awareness · **Source:** Sector's real 6-category buying-signal framework

> Most companies can't tell the difference between a lead and a signal.
>
> A lead is someone who raised a hand. A signal is evidence that something has changed inside a company — and it usually arrives long before the hand goes up.
>
> Six categories worth watching:
>
> 1. **Internal** — leadership changes, restructures, new roles opening in revenue functions
> 2. **Market** — funding rounds, category shifts, regulation, consolidation
> 3. **Behavioral** — what they're actually doing: hiring patterns, tooling changes, what they publish
> 4. **Stated** — what they've said out loud in interviews, reports, job specs, earnings calls
> 5. **Competitive** — a competitor moving, winning, or failing loudly
> 6. **Predictive** — the patterns that historically precede a buying decision in that sector
>
> Most teams work only category 4, because it's the only one that arrives in the inbox.
>
> The compounding advantage is in 1 and 3. New leadership means new budget and new tolerance for change. Hiring patterns tell you what someone has decided to fix before they've told anyone.
>
> None of this requires a tool. It requires deciding the signals are worth reading.
>
> Which of the six do you actually track today?
